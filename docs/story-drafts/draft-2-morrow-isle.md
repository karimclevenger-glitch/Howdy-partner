# Story Draft 2: "Morrow Isle" (working title)

> **Status:** DRAFT 2, built on **your** concept: an island, a time loop, a bird-headmistress who resets it, a house full of
> unusual children, invisible monsters, building glasses to see them, and preparing for a final battle you can lose.
> Nothing is locked until you say so. Mark it up however you like.

---

## Your idea, played back to you (so you know I got it)

You travel to an island because you read about it. On the island you find a way into a **time loop**: the same day,
repeating. Inside is an old house run by a **headmistress who can turn into a bird** and who **resets the loop** every
day to protect the **unusual children** in her care. You explore the house, find items, and get to know the children.
Monsters are coming. Unlike everyone else, **you can't see them**, so you have to **build special glasses** and
**make weapons** out of what you find. Everything you explore, everyone you talk to, and everything you collect leaves
you **better prepared for the final battle**. If you rush and skip things, **you can lose.**

That's a genuinely great game design, and it's *more* game-shaped than my first draft. I've built everything below
around it.

## About Miss Peregrine (your question, and one honest line)

**The bird:** Miss Peregrine turns into a **peregrine falcon**, and that's where her name comes from. In the books, the bird-women who
make and keep the time loops are called **ymbrynes**, and the monsters are **hollowgast** ("hollows").

**The honest line:** *Miss Peregrine's Home for Peculiar Children* belongs to its author, Ransom Riggs (and the film to its
studio), the same way King's Quest belongs to its owners. So I won't copy its characters, names, specific powers or plot.
The premise you love (an island, a loop, a protective bird-headmistress, gifted children, invisible monsters) is made of
ideas, though, and ideas are fair game. So below is **our own house, our own headmistress, our own children, our own monsters**,
with the same eerie, wonderful *feeling*.

There's a game reason too: if our children were the book's children, you'd already know every one of them and every twist.
Original ones mean you get to be surprised by your own game.

## The pitch

**Morrow Isle** is a windswept island in a cold northern sea where nothing much happens anymore. You've come because of a
bundle of old letters you found tucked inside a second-hand book. They're dated 1899, they're **addressed to you by
name**, and the last one says:

> *"At the turning of the tide, knock three times on the door that isn't there. Please hurry. You're the only one who can
> build the spectacles. — W."*

On the cliff, among the weeds, there's a lone doorframe with no house behind it. You knock: *tap, tap… boom.*
The door opens onto **Tuesday, the 17th of October, 1899**, and a great crooked house full of light, where eight
unusual children have been living the same Tuesday for **one hundred and twenty-six years**.

**They were expecting you.** And by coming, you've accidentally brought something dangerous in with you.

## The rules of the loop

1. **Every evening at dusk the day rewinds.** The headmistress turns into a **nightjar**, a dusk-flying bird whose
   strange churring call sounds like a clock being wound. She circles the house, and Tuesday morning comes again. Spilled
   milk un-spills, broken windows un-break, and the same rain shower falls at 11:15.
2. **The household remembers.** The headmistress and the children know it's the same day. They just never age.
3. **The island doesn't remember.** The 1899 villagers live Tuesday fresh every time and do the same things at the same
   minutes. You can learn their routines and use them.
4. **You remember, and so does your satchel.** Anything you're carrying when the day rewinds stays with you.
5. **The children's nights never come.** None of them has seen a night sky in 126 years.
6. **Why the loop exists:** the monsters, called **Haunters**, hunt by smelling *tomorrows*. A day that never reaches tomorrow
   leaves no trail, so the house has been invisible to them for over a century.
7. **The catch:** *you* came from the future. You carry tomorrow on you like the smell of rain. Now the Haunters can follow
   it, and in three days they'll break through.

---

## What you DO (the game, moment to moment)

- **Explore the house and the island.** The house is the heart of the game: attic, kitchens, library, nursery, the
  headmistress's study, the greenhouse, the boathouse, the cellar under the cellar. Outside are the 1899 village,
  the lighthouse, the sea caves and the graveyard.
- **Talk to everyone.** Each child has a gift, a personality, and **a problem only you can help with**. Helping them earns
  their trust, and trust is what turns into weapons and allies on the final night.
- **Collect and combine items** in classic point-and-click style: things found, traded, earned and built.
- **Build the Seeing Spectacles.** This is the main quest, and the parts come from all over the house (details below).
- **Prepare for the siege:** make weapons, set traps, and decide which child guards which part of the house.
- **Survive the Last Tuesday,** when the loop fails, night falls on the house for the first time in 126 years, and the
  Haunters come.
- **Die in eerie-funny ways along the way.** Every death plays out, gets a narrator line, and offers **Try Again**.

## The cast

### You
A teenager from the present day who found the letters. **The letters use your name**, because the game asks for it at the start.
*(Question for you: do you want to play a girl, a boy, or choose at the start? Choosing means drawing and animating
two main characters, which roughly doubles the hardest art job.)*

### The headmistress: Miss Ottilie Fenwick ("the Nightjar")
Tall, sharp-eyed, with a dress the mottled grey-brown of tree bark, because nightjars are so well camouflaged they're
nearly invisible. Stern on the surface and fiercely loving underneath. She's kept the loop going for 126 years, she is very
tired, and she's **not happy you're here**, because your arrival put her children in danger. Winning her trust is part of the game.
Her fear: that the loop is a cage, and she's been keeping the children safe by keeping them from ever growing up.

### The children (who call themselves "the Oddments")
Each child's gift is also a **puzzle tool**, and helping each one gets you something for the final night.

Bossy, brave, carries everyone. | She's never been allowed to make her own decisions. Prove she can lead. | On the final night, **the house fights back**: doors slam on Haunters and the stairs fold up. |

*(That's eight children, which is a good size. More means less depth per child.)*

### The villain: the Tallyman
The Haunters aren't wild animals. They're **bailiffs**, and they answer to **the Tallyman**: tall, thin and impeccably polite,
in a stovepipe hat, with a ledger chained to his wrist and an abacus whose beads click like teeth. He keeps the accounts
of time itself.

> *"Every day must be paid for, madam. You have borrowed the same Tuesday for one hundred and twenty-six years.
> With interest. I am here to collect."*

What does he collect? **Tomorrows.** Everyone the Haunters catch "pays" all their skipped days at once. They fade like an
old photograph left in the sun, until there's nothing left. *(Eerie, but no gore.)*

### The Haunters
Invisible without the spectacles. Seen through the lenses, they're tall, crooked silhouettes stitched from fog and the
insides of broken clocks. **They're drawn to ticking**, because ticking is time passing, and that's what they eat.
Players will learn to use that against them.

### The islanders of 1899 (who live Tuesday fresh every time)
The lighthouse keeper who drinks at exactly 2:00, the baker whose bread comes out at 3:00, the vicar who rings the
church bell at 4:00, and the fisherman who tells the same lie about a sea monster every afternoon. They're a small,
funny supporting cast, and their routines are puzzles.

## The structure: three Tuesdays and a night

### Prologue: "The Door That Isn't There" (present day)
The ferry to the island, the near-empty modern village, the legend of "the house where the children vanished in 1899,"
the cliff, and the doorframe. You knock (tap, tap… boom), and step into 1899.
*Teaches:* walking, looking, items, talking.

### Day One: "The First Tuesday"
You arrive mid-morning. The children are thrilled ("You're late!"). Miss Fenwick is cold as the sea. You explore the house,
meet everyone, and learn about the loop, the Haunters, and the old legend of the **Seeing Spectacles** that a long-ago
headmistress once made. **Wren hears the memory of them in the walls.** At dusk, you see the reset for the first time: the
nightjar circling, the children clapping their rhyme in the hall, and the whole world winding backwards.
Then something you can't see leaves frosted **handprints on the outside of the nursery window.** They've found the house.

### Day Two: "The Second Tuesday"
This is the big exploration day. You learn the islanders' routines, help the children, and gather the spectacle parts. **The
first time you put the spectacles on is a showpiece moment.** You see the house as it really is, and something tall is
standing very still at the end of the garden, looking straight at you.
At dusk the reset happens, but more weakly. Miss Fenwick comes back from her flight with a torn wing. **The loop is failing.**

### Day Three: "The Last Tuesday"
Preparation day. You build weapons, set traps, finish side quests, and decide who guards which door. **You choose when
you're ready** by ringing the dinner bell to begin the night. Ring it early and you face the night less prepared.

### The Siege
For the first time in 126 years, **the sun goes down on the house and doesn't come back up.** The children see stars.
Then the Haunters come through the walls, and the Tallyman knocks politely at the front door.

The siege plays in **stages** (front door, upstairs, cellar, the roof), and **what you prepared decides how each stage
goes**. Barricades you magnet-bolted hold. Rooms with mirrors let Ada warn you where the Haunters are coming from. If Clem trusts
you, the house fights back. If you never befriended Pip, nobody can steal the Tallyman's ledger.

**The final confrontation** has **several ways to win**, depending on what you prepared. Hettie can age the ledger to
dust. The twins can trap the Tallyman in a mirror. Or you, with a pen from the future, can find the loophole in his
contract. Wren will have told you about it, if you listened.

### Endings
- **Best ending:** everyone survives, the debt is settled, and the children choose to step out of the loop and let time
  move again. The last thing you hear is the whole house shrieking with joy: **"IT'S WEDNESDAY!"**
- **Middle endings:** you win, but some children were "collected" because you weren't prepared enough to protect them.
  It's bittersweet, and you can replay the Last Tuesday to do better.
- **Losing:** the house falls. **But it's a time-loop game, so losing isn't the end.** With the last of her strength, Miss
  Fenwick winds back one more Tuesday. You wake on the morning of Day Three **with everything you've learned and everything
  in your satchel**, ready to prepare properly. *(More on this choice below.)*

### Epilogue: "Dear ___"
Back in the present day, you sit down at a desk with an old pen and a stack of 1899 notepaper, and you realize you know
exactly what to write. **The letters that brought you to the island were in your own handwriting all along.**
You seal the last one: *"Please hurry. You're the only one who can build the spectacles."*

## The main quest: the Seeing Spectacles

A first sketch of the parts and where they come from (the final design comes in the detailed chapter pass):

```
SEEING SPECTACLES
├─ FRAMES ───── the old headmistress's wire frames, hidden somewhere in the house
│                └─ Wren hears where (once you've helped her sleep)
├─ LENSES ───── glass that has "seen the other side": slivers of the twins' mirror
│                └─ Ada will only part with it after you get the twins their hug
├─ THE FOG ──── Ezra's fog, sealed between the lens layers ("they show up in fog…")
│                └─ he'll bottle it once someone finally applauds his magic show
├─ THE SCREWS ─ far too tiny to handle; Silas's magnetism holds them while you work
└─ THE FEATHER  a nightjar feather to "wake" the lenses
                 └─ Miss Fenwick gives it only when she finally trusts you
```

This is required: you can't win without the spectacles, so the game makes sure you finish them by the end of Day Two.
**Weapons, traps and allies are optional**, and they're what decide how hard the siege is and which ending you get.

## How "being prepared" works (and the one thing I changed)

You asked for it to be possible to reach the final boss unprepared and **fail**. Yes, absolutely. That tension is what makes
preparing feel meaningful.

The one thing I'd change is the old-school version where you lose 10 hours because you missed an item on Day One. I'd
make the **loop itself** the safety net: lose the siege, and you rewind to the morning of the Last Tuesday with everything
you've learned and everything you're carrying. **Failing turns into knowledge** ("Now I know they come through the cellar"),
which is exactly what a time-loop story is about. You can always fail. You can never get permanently stuck.

*(If you'd rather have harsher stakes, where losing the siege means a bad ending and a reload, that's your call. See the
questions below.)*

## Where's the King's Quest in this?
Still here: point-and-click exploration, inventory puzzles with a whimsical twist, eccentric characters who are
puzzles themselves, a narrator with a dry sense of humor, comic-eerie deaths with Try Again, and lots of multiple
solutions in the finale. What's new is the time loop, the preparation-matters siege, and the multiple endings.

## Why this is a great fit for us (honestly)
It's **much more achievable** than my first draft. That story needed six completely different regions. This one is
**one house and one island, seen across three days, at dusk and at night.** The same painted rooms get reused with
different lighting, which I can do in code. That's roughly **20–25 backgrounds instead of 45–50**, so the art can be better
and the game is more likely to actually get finished.

## The rhyme (keeping your repo's name alive)
Every evening, while Miss Fenwick winds the day back, the children stand in the hall and clap:

> *Tap-tap, chicka boom boom,*
> *Tuesday's back in every room.*
> *Tap-tap, chicka boom bay,*
> *Haunters never find the way.*
> *Wind it back and wind it tight,*
> *Never, never comes the night.*
> *Tap-tap, chicka boom boom,*
> *Good morning, Morrow! Make some room!*

In the best ending, they change the last line: *"Here comes Wednesday! Make some room!"*
